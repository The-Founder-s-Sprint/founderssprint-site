Investment Readiness · Week 1Why funding strategy matters
A funding strategy connects the money you raise to the milestones you need to hit.The Funding Strategy Handbook

Most founders treat fundraising as one event: 'we need money.' In reality it's a sequence of decisions that shape who owns your company, who sits at your table, and what you're obligated to deliver.

Get it wrong and it's expensive: take equity too early and you give away too much; chase every grant and you burn months; raise without a milestone plan and you run out of cash halfway to the proof that would have justified the next round.

Investment Readiness · Week 1The capital map
The single most useful framing

Every source of money is one of two families.

Non-dilutive
You keep all your ownership.
Sources
Grants, competition prizes, accelerator stipends, revenue, debt.
Best when
You're early, want to preserve ownership, or are proving a concept.
The catch
Slow, restricted in use, competitive, often one-off.
Dilutive
You sell a share of the company.
Sources
Angels, venture capital, equity crowdfunding, accelerator cheques.
Best when
You need large amounts to grow fast and can offer a high return.
The catch
You permanently own less; investors expect an exit.

The insight: your equity is worth the least when you're earliest — so use non-dilutive capital first to push the company's value up before you sell any ownership.

Investment Readiness · Week 1L3-4.1.2 · the East African correction
The global playbook needs three local fixes

Fundraising in Kampala is not fundraising in California.

Capital is thin & lumpy

A local seed round isn't waiting for you. Expect to look regionally and pair small cheques with grants.

Grants do the heavy lifting

~75% of African startup funding is pre-Series-A, much of it non-dilutive. TEF, Google BFF, Hi-Innovator wrote thousands of 'first cheques.'

Most 'VC' is really DFI money

IFC, BII, Proparco, FMO are the largest source of venture capital on the continent — they fund the local funds that fund you.

90% of disclosed investment has historically gone to startups with at least one Western founder. Knowing the real landscape is your edge.

Investment Readiness · Week 1Where the money lives
The East African map

Know the players before you knock.

Hubs & accelerators

Innovation Village / MoTIV, Outbox, Hive Colab, StartHub, Founder Institute.

Grant programmes

Hi-Innovator (NSSF), Tony Elumelu, Google BFF, Mastercard Foundation, sector challenge funds.

Angels & micro-VC

KAIN locally, ABAN regionally; pan-African micro-VCs writing USD 25k–250k.

Funds & the DFIs behind them

EA-focused & pan-African funds — largely capitalised by IFC, BII, FMO & peers.

Research current mandates before approaching anyone — a fund's stage, sector and geography change every cycle.

Case Study
🇺🇬 From first grant to global round

Use the cheapest capital first.

Start non-dilutive

A Hi-Innovator or TEF grant funds the prototype and first proof — zero ownership given up.

Stack the wins

Each grant or competition win is independent validation that de-risks the next conversation.

Then sell equity high

Chipper Cash sequenced accelerator → pre-seed → Series rounds, raising equity once the company was worth far more.

Sequenced well, non-dilutive capital builds the value of the company before any equity is ever sold.

Investment Readiness · Week 1The strategy = a sequence
It all comes together as an order

A funding strategy is a deliberate sequence.

1

Bootstrap + grants

Prove the concept on the cheapest, non-dilutive money.

2

Pre-seed

A little equity (SAFE) to reach clear proof points.

3

Seed

Equity to turn proof into a repeatable business.

4

Series A +

Scale what works — growth VCs and DFIs, direct.

Each step should raise the company's value and lower its risk before the next. The principle is simple: use the cheapest capital first.
