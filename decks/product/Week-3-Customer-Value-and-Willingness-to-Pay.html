Product & Pricing · Week 3Benefits received − costs incurred
Value is felt, not calculated

Customers buy the gap between benefit and cost.

Value isn't what your product does — it's what the customer gains: time saved, convenience, status, revenue grown, cost cut, risk removed. Subtract what it costs them (money, effort, switching) and what's left is perceived value.

Raise the benefit they feel, and the price they'll accept rises with it.

The value equation

Value = Benefits received − Costs incurred. Price toward the benefit, not your effort.

Product & Pricing · Week 3Different customers, different ceilings
One price rarely fits everyone

Willingness to pay is personal — so segment it.

Students

Low WTP — tight budgets, many free alternatives. Reach with entry tiers.

Professionals

Medium WTP — value time and convenience, will pay for quality.

Corporates

High WTP — buy outcomes and risk reduction, least price-sensitive.

The same coffee is UGX 2,000 at a kiosk and UGX 12,000 in a hotel lounge. Context and segment set the ceiling — not the beans.

Product & Pricing · Week 3Six ways to find the number
From a quick conversation to a controlled test

Six ways to learn what they'll pay.

Surveys & interviews

Ask directly — fast and cheap, but people over-state. Pair with behaviour.

Van Westendorp

Four price questions map the psychologically acceptable range.

Conjoint analysis

Forced trade-offs reveal what each feature — and price — is really worth.

A/B testing

Show different prices to similar customers; let real choices decide.

Market experiments

Launch small at a price, measure take-up, adjust, repeat.

The rule

Watch what people do, not only what they say.
